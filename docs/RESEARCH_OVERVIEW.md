## 1. The research in one sentence

We test whether phishing-email detectors wrongly flag **legitimate emails written with AI help**. If they do, we find out *why*: the amount of AI assistance, AI-authored content, or simply the email coming from a different source than the training data. We also test whether any fix reduces these false alarms **without letting real or AI-written phishing through**.

Working title: *"Is it the AI or the inbox? Graded AI assistance, AI authorship and source shift as causes of phishing-detector false alarms on legitimate email."*

## 2. Why this research is needed

1. **AI-written email is now normal on both sides.** At least about 51% of spam was LLM-generated by April 2025 (Hao et al., IMC 2025). Legitimate senders use the same tools to proofread, polish or draft their mail.
2. **False alarms have real costs.** A legitimate email flagged as phishing can be quarantined or ignored. That means lost business mail, more analyst workload and alert fatigue. Users also learn to distrust warnings.
3. **There is a fairness risk.** People who use AI to fix their English, often non-native speakers, may be penalised. This mirrors the bias already documented for AI-text detectors (Liang et al., Patterns 2023).
4. **Research has looked almost only at the attack side.** Most studies ask "does AI-written phishing evade detectors?" Few ask "do detectors over-flag AI-assisted *legitimate* mail?"
5. **Fixes can backfire.** Teaching a detector that "AI-sounding equals benign" would hand attackers a bypass. Any mitigation has to be judged from both sides.

## 3. The gap (what prior work has not done)

| Gap | Evidence it is open |
|---|---|
| **G1: benign-side measurement.** False alarms on AI-written legitimate email are rarely measured, and never at frozen operating thresholds with confidence intervals | Only small commercial-filter tests exist: Yahoo flagged 58–66% of legitimate AI emails (Opara et al., ESWA 2025). Otherwise there are accuracy figures for LLM judges (Zhang et al., IEEE Access 2025) |
| **G2: style versus source confound.** Studies of AI emails compare different datasets, so AI writing and dataset shift move together | E-PhishGEN 2025, Alisoy 2026 and EdgeGuard 2026 all compare across sources or generators |
| **G3: no dose–response.** Nobody has measured how much AI help (proofread, polish, rewrite, compose) it takes before phishing detectors start to misfire | APT-Eval (ACL Findings 2025) did this for *AI-text detectors* only |
| **G4: one-sided mitigation evaluation.** Fixes are reported as accuracy gains within their own pipeline. Nobody checks whether they let modern or AI-written phishing through | Ferrell 2025, Nance 2025 and Tran-Truong 2027 report augmentation within the pipeline |
| **G5: reproducibility.** Split leakage and missing code are common | CyberCane 2026 found a 96% leaky split, and E-PhishGEN notes missing code |

## 4. What our early data already show (Study 1, exploratory)

- **Paraphrasing barely matters in-distribution.** GPT-4o, DeepSeek and a third provider rewrote the D2 emails. At most 1 of 142 legitimate decisions changed (exact McNemar p = 1).
- **A new source of AI-written legitimate mail causes severe false alarms.** On E-PhishLLM, 77–97% of legitimate emails were flagged at thresholds set for 5% FPR, while phishing recall stayed at about 99%.
- **The ranking survives; the threshold does not.** AUROC is about 0.95, yet the frozen threshold fails. This is a threshold-transfer problem.
- **Every quick fix traded FPR against recall.**

Study 1 cannot say *why*, because AI authorship and source were confounded. Study 2 is designed to answer that.

## 5. Contributions (Study 2, confirmatory and pre-registered)

- **C1. A graded AI-assistance benchmark.** The same real legitimate email is produced at five levels:
  - L0: human original
  - L1: proofread
  - L2: polish
  - L3: full rewrite
  - L4: composed from the same subject line

  URLs, email addresses and phone numbers are locked, so the security-relevant evidence is byte-identical. The legitimate-only part will be released.
- **C2. A factorial separation of causes.** The amount of assistance (L0–L3), AI-authored content (L4) and source shift (in-distribution D2, external Enron, external Clinton FOIA) are separated with paired statistics.
- **C3. The mechanism.** We decompose each failure into ranking loss versus threshold transfer, and run controlled cue interventions (adding or removing a URL, a signature, a footer or a greeting).
- **C4. Two-sided mitigation evaluation.** Six pre-specified mitigations are each judged on:
  - false alarms on AI-written legitimate mail;
  - recall on real phishing from 2023–2025 (Nazario);
  - recall on AI-rewritten phishing, reported as "attacker benefit".
- **C5. An open, pre-registered, leakage-audited protocol.** Hashes, a locked test manifest, a tested shared library (phishbench), a REFORMS checklist and a Zenodo DOI.

## 6. Novelty statement

> To our knowledge, this is the first study that measures a **dose–response of AI writing assistance** on phishing-detector false alarms using **paired, placeholder-locked versions of the same real legitimate emails**. It **separates** assistance, AI-authored content and source shift, and it evaluates mitigations **two-sidedly** against modern real phishing and AI-rewritten phishing under a **pre-registered** protocol.

"To our knowledge" stays in the paper, and the claim is re-checked right before submission.

## 7. What we do NOT claim (honest prior art)

These are cited, not claimed:
- LLM rephrasing degrades phishing detectors (Afane 2024; Ferrell 2025; SoK 2025; EdgeGuard 2026).
- Cross-corpus drops and corpus-artifact learning (E-PhishGEN 2025; Bhuiyan & Bhuiyan 2026).
- Thresholds not transferring across sources (CyberCane 2026; Litvak 2026).
- LLM-rewrite augmentation (Ferrell 2025; Nance 2025; Tran-Truong 2027).
- LLM-as-detector (Koide 2026; Zhang 2025).
- Graded AI-polish evaluation for AI-text detectors (APT-Eval 2025). We transfer that *design* to phishing detection.
- Four-cell human/LLM × phishing/legitimate corpora (Greco et al. 2024; PhishFuzzer 2025). We add the paired, graded design and frozen-threshold false-alarm analysis.

## 8. Possible results and what the paper will say (decided in advance)

| Result | Headline |
|---|---|
| False alarms rise with the level of AI assistance (PE0 trend) | "Even light AI polishing raises phishing false alarms". The level where the rise begins tells AI-assisted senders where the risk starts |
| Flat across L0–L3, with a jump only at L4 | "AI-authored content, not AI editing, triggers false alarms" |
| Flat everywhere, but high on external sources | "It is the inbox, not the AI: source shift drives false alarms" |
| A mitigation passes all criteria | A recommended, safe fix with its documented trade-off |
| No mitigation passes | A documented trade-off frontier and a warning against one-sided fixes |

Every row is a publishable, useful finding. That is why the study suits PLOS ONE, which judges rigour and validity rather than "positive results".

## 9. Why PLOS ONE

- Its publication criteria are original research, rigorous methods, conclusions supported by data, ethics and data availability. It does not judge perceived impact, and it accepts null results.
- Our design matches those criteria: pre-registration, leakage audits, equivalence tests, open code and a clear data-access route.
- Optionally, it offers a **Registered Report** route, which gives in-principle acceptance before the confirmatory results are known. See the route table in the plan.
